# Consciousness and Discontinuous Time

## Status and scope

This document develops a philosophical and conceptual framework prompted by three propositions:

1. Conscious awareness may be intermittent rather than phenomenally continuous.
2. The physical passage of time need not match the passage of experienced or remembered time.
3. Death can be described metaphorically as a "skip through time" from the standpoint of an observer who has no experience of the intervening interval.

These propositions are not equivalent. The first two have useful connections to neuroscience and philosophy of time; the third is an analogy, not evidence that consciousness resumes after irreversible death. This note distinguishes the model from established findings and from claims that remain unknown.

## 1. Three different timelines

Let physical time be represented by a parameter (t). Let (C(t)) denote the presence and degree of conscious experience, and let (M(t)) denote accessible memory.

It is useful to distinguish:

- **Physical duration:** elapsed time measured by clocks and physical processes.
- **Experienced duration:** duration as it is phenomenally perceived.
- **Remembered duration:** duration reconstructed from accessible memory.

These quantities can diverge. A person emerging from dreamless sleep may report that no time seemed to pass, even though hours elapsed. That report concerns remembered and experienced duration; it does not imply that physical time stopped.

A schematic expression is

[
d\tau = \phi(C(t),X(t))\,dt,
]

where \(\tau\) is a model of subjective temporal accumulation and \(X(t)\) collects relevant cognitive conditions such as attention, arousal, and temporal processing. The function \(\phi\) is not known to be a universal measurable law. In intervals without experience, assigning a subjective duration may simply be inapplicable rather than equal to zero.

## 2. Intermittency and continuity

A stream of consciousness can be modelled as a sequence of experiential states

[
C_0,C_1,\ldots,C_n.
]

A transition model might take the form

[
C_{n+1}=F(C_n,S_{n+1},M_n),
]

where (S_{n+1}) represents new sensory and internal input, and (M_n) represents retained information.

The sequence may feel continuous because successive states overlap, integrate information, and preserve short-term context. This does not establish that consciousness consists of isolated, discrete frames. The sequence is a useful abstraction; the temporal grain and continuity of experience remain empirical and philosophical questions.

Likewise, periods of absent report or absent memory should not automatically be equated with a total absence of consciousness. Sleep, anesthesia, and other altered states differ, and retrospective reports are imperfect evidence of what was experienced.

## 3. Memory and personal identity

Memory helps connect experiences across time, but identity cannot be reduced to autobiographical memory alone. People remain the same person through forgetting, changing beliefs, and changes in personality.

A schematic identity update is

[
I_{n+1}=G(I_n,M_{n+1},B_{n+1},R_{n+1}),
]

where (B) represents bodily continuity and (R) represents psychological and social relationships. This is a conceptual representation, not a criterion that settles personal identity.

The key distinction is between:

- continuity of the living organism;
- continuity of psychological processes;
- continuity of accessible memory;
- the felt sense of being one persisting subject.

These can support one another without being identical.

## 4. The "skip through time" analogy

For reversible unconsciousness, an individual may have no remembered interval between the last moment before sleep and waking. From the first-person retrospective perspective, the interval can seem compressed or absent.

Death is different. Irreversible death is not simply a longer sleep: the biological conditions that support consciousness cease permanently. The fact that an interval is not experienced does not imply that a later experience will occur, nor that the same subject will awaken after it.

Thus the phrase **"death is a skip through time"** can be retained as a description of the imagined first-person absence of an interval, but it must not be presented as a demonstrated account of death or an afterlife. There is no established evidence that personal consciousness resumes after irreversible death.

## 5. A layered state model

For conceptual bookkeeping, distinguish the following variables:

- (P(t)): physical and bodily state;
- (A(t)): conscious activity, if present;
- (M(t)): memory state;
- (I(t)): identity-related state;
- (T(t)): physical time.

A schematic dependency structure is

[
P(t)\longrightarrow A(t)\longrightarrow M(t)\longrightarrow I(t),
]

with feedback: memory and identity influence later attention, interpretation, and action, which in turn affect the body's interaction with the world.

One possible abstract update system is

[
\begin{aligned}
P_{n+1}&=f(P_n,T_n,U_n),\\
A_{n+1}&=g(P_{n+1},A_n),\\
M_{n+1}&=h(M_n,A_{n+1}),\\
I_{n+1}&=j(I_n,M_{n+1},P_{n+1}).
\end{aligned}
]

Here (U_n) represents environmental and internal influences. These functions are placeholders for processes that would need operational definitions before the model could be tested.

## 6. What the framework does and does not claim

The framework supports several modest conclusions:

1. Physical elapsed time and remembered duration are not the same quantity.
2. A person can lack an accessible memory of an interval without physical time having stopped.
3. Memory contributes to the felt continuity of a self, but does not alone explain personal identity.
4. The absence of experience during an interval does not logically entail a later return of experience.
5. Consciousness, its neural conditions, and subjective time remain active scientific and philosophical questions.

It does **not** establish that consciousness is literally switched off between discrete moments, that time is created by an observer, or that death is followed by renewed experience.

## 7. Open questions

The framework suggests testable and philosophical questions:

- How do attention, arousal, and memory encoding shape estimates of duration?
- Which neural processes support temporal integration and the feeling of continuity?
- Can reports of no remembered interval distinguish absent experience from failed memory formation?
- What forms of bodily, psychological, and social continuity are relevant to identity?
- Is subjective time best modelled as continuous accumulation, event ordering, or a combination?

## Conclusion

The central distinction is

[
\boxed{\text{physical existence}\neq\text{conscious experience}\neq\text{remembered continuity}.}
]

A moment need not be remembered to have occurred, and an unremembered interval need not be an interval in which nothing happened. Consciousness may be intermittent in some senses, while the world and the living organism continue through time. Death makes the analogy especially evocative, but also marks its limit: an absence of experienced duration cannot establish a future awakening.

The framework is therefore best treated as a map of distinctions and questions, not as a completed theory of consciousness or death.
